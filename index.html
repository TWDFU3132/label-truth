Is there human DNA in food? What does the FDA allow?

Short answer: The FDA has no allowed amount of human DNA or human hair in food. Its Food Defect Levels Handbook lists allowed amounts of bug pieces, rodent hairs, mold, fly eggs, maggots, mites, animal poop ("mammalian excreta") and grit. Nothing human is on the list.

Where the "human DNA" idea comes from: In 2015 a company called Clear Food tested 345 hot dogs and sausages and reported human DNA in 2% of them (6 samples), 4 of which were vegetarian. They said it most likely came from hair, skin, fingernails or saliva during manufacturing. It was a company report, not a peer-reviewed study, and it was never repeated at scale.

The real "human" ingredient: L-cysteine, a dough softener in some breads, bagels and pizza crusts, has historically been made from human hair (mostly in China), and also from duck feathers and hog hair. Today some is made by fermentation. The label never says which. This app flags it.

What "allowed" means: Defect levels are the point where the FDA takes legal action. Most food has far less. The FDA says these natural defects are unavoidable and "pose no inherent hazard to health," and that companies can't blend bad batches with good ones to get under the limit.
